Where This Model Breaks

An honest inventory of the weakest assumptions in the Alaska Airlines decarbonization explorer — the things most likely to be wrong, and the things that would change the answer if they were.

Companion to docs/variables.html

Why this document exists. The engine reproduces the source workbook to rel=1e-9, which makes it a verified port. It does not make it a good model. Parity guarantees the arithmetic; it guarantees nothing about the assumptions. The items below are ordered by how badly they would damage a conclusion drawn from this tool.

Fatal — the result does not survive these

1. The residual cannot actually be boughtfatal

Every scenario reaches "net zero" in 2040 because neutralization_share = 1.00 neutralizes the whole residual at the planning price. That is an accounting entry, not a procurement plan.

All-In 2040 residual 3.52 Mt Conservative 12.65 Mt Whole observed durable removal market 1.88 Mt

Alaska alone would need roughly twice the entire observed durable removal market on the All-In path, and seven times it on the Conservative one — for one airline carrying about 3% of US capacity. The app's own carbon-market tab says as much when growth is left at 0%, then the model charges the purchase anyway and reports net zero.

Effect: the phrase "net zero 2040" in this tool means "we priced the residual", not "we could buy it". Any cost comparison that leans on offsets being available at the planning price is unsafe.

2. The SAF buy-down has no floorfatal

SAF premium(t) = market premium(t) x (1 - 0.01) ^ (cumulative CVC(t) / $50M)

Nothing bounds this below. Push catalytic capital to its 5% slider maximum and $13.80B of cumulative investment drives the multiplier to 0.0624:

SAF $3.16/gal vs jet $2.97/gal — a 6% premium SAF abatement $20/t

SAF has a cost of production that cannot fall to near-parity with fossil jet fuel no matter how much capital arrives. The curve should asymptote to a floor; it decays to zero.

Effect: every conclusion at high catalytic_pct is fantasy. The 2% used by All-In is already a 67% premium cut, which is aggressive.

3. r = 1% per $50M is inventedfatal

The learning rate and its $50M tranche have no citation, no literature anchor and no empirical basis anywhere in this repo. They were chosen in conversation. They are the single largest determinant of whether investing early pays off:

Rate2040 SAF priceDoes All-In beat Conservative?
0 (no buy-down)$324/tNo, and it never can
1% / $50M (as shipped)$107/tYes, by $6.48B

Effect: the headline finding is a function of an unsourced parameter. This is the first number to attack, and the first to replace with something defensible.

4. Alaska takes a quarter of US SAF supplyfatal

All-In 2040: 1.22B gal of 4.98B gal available — 24.4%

Alaska is roughly 3% of US airline capacity. The model lets it consume eight times its proportional share because there are no other buyers in it. There is no competition for supply, no price response to demand, and no allocation mechanism. The supply constraint binds only against Alaska's own appetite.

Effect: All-In's physical pathway is not feasible in a market containing other airlines. It describes a world where Alaska is the only bidder.

Circular — the model assumes part of its own conclusion

5. Alaska's investment discounts only Alaska's pricecircular

Catalytic capital builds public SAF capacity, then hands Alaska alone the resulting price decline. In reality, capacity investment lowers the market clearing price for every buyer, and the investor captures a share roughly proportional to its own purchases — a few percent, not all of it.

So the mechanism reads: spend money → your own input price falls → you look efficient. The benefit of a public good is booked privately, with no spillover and no free-riding by competitors.

Effect: the return on catalytic capital is overstated by roughly the reciprocal of Alaska's market share. This is the most structurally circular part of the model.

6. The carbon escalation rate was reverse-engineeredcircular

Both strategies use carbon_escalation = 3.3%/yr. That rate was not taken from a forecast. It was solved for: it is the rate that lifts $200/t to SAF's undiscounted $324/t by 2040 — that is, the rate that makes the two price lines cross.

Effect: the crossing is partly assumed rather than discovered. 3.3%/yr is not implausible for carbon prices, which is the only reason this is uncomfortable rather than disqualifying.

Framing — the same model gives opposite answers

7. Intensity can improve while emissions riseframing

The 2030 target is an intensity reduction per revenue tonne mile. Under business as usual the model reports:

intensity 15.2% better by 2040 absolute emissions 10.46 → 13.54 Mt (+29.4%)

Growth outruns efficiency. The headline KPI tile can read green while the airline emits a third more carbon than when it started.

Effect: "meets target" is not "is decarbonizing". The app shows absolute tonnes on the second chart, but the 2030 verdict is intensity-only because the target is.

Effect: a reader could take either conclusion from this repo depending on which column they read. The choice is stated in the docs rather than buried, but it is doing enormous work.

Gaps — things simply not modeled

9. Three levers are freegap

Fleet renewal, ground electrification and novel propulsion carry zero capex. Take All-In, strip its operational levers out, then put fleet renewal at 50% and ground electrification at 100% back in:

no ops levers: $46.22B, 76.1% reduction fleet 50% + ground 100%: $44.11B, 78.5% reduction

Cheaper and more abatement, because the levers cut fuel burn and therefore the SAF volume that has to be bought, while costing nothing to install. That is an artifact of the missing capital cost, not a finding. Any optimizer run against this model maxes all three immediately.

10. Excluded costs and scopesgap

ExcludedWhy it matters
Scope 3 emissionsThe large majority of an airline's value-chain footprint
CORSIA compliance costA mandatory, real cost. The $59/t midpoint appears as a reference and is never charged
Fleet / ground / propulsion capexSee above
Fuel-price pass-through to faresRevenue tracks activity only, so a fuel shock cuts margin with no pricing response

11. SAF lifecycle reduction is fixed at 80%gap

One constant for all SAF, at any volume, from any feedstock. Real pathways range from roughly 50% to 90%, and the cheap high-performing feedstocks are the first to be exhausted. At All-In's 1.22B gal/yr, the marginal gallon would almost certainly perform worse than the first. The model has no feedstock quality curve and no indirect land-use effect.

Effect: every SAF abatement figure is optimistic at high volume, and tonnes_avoided_per_saf_gallon — which divides every cost-per-tonne in the app — inherits the error.

12. Partner share has no ceiling and no double-count checkgap

partner_share shifts premium cost to third parties, up to 100%, with nothing verifying that those parties exist, that the volume is available, or that the same tonne is not also claimed by the corporate buyer under a book-and-claim scheme. At 100% the SAF is free to Alaska and the abatement is still counted as Alaska's.

13. A hidden input still drives an outputgap

investable_pct was removed from the sidebar and pinned at 3%. It no longer appears anywhere on the page, but it still governs financial_2040's cash-headroom verdict and viz.budget_bar. A live judgement rests on a number no user can see or change.

Numerical

14. Band classification is precision-blind near the edge

A genuine 9.99% reduction displays as "10.0%" but is classified below target, producing the label "Below target (10.0% vs 10% floor)". Only the float-noise case is handled, by a 1e-9 tolerance added so that a pathway solved to land exactly on the floor — which arrives at 0.09999999999999998 — is not reported as missing it.

Effect: cosmetic, but the label can contradict the number beside it.

If you only fix three things

1. Put a floor under the SAF buy-down at a defensible cost of production, and source the learning rate. These two together decide the headline finding.

2. Cap Alaska's market capture at something near its share of US capacity, or state loudly that the pathway assumes no competing demand.

3. Decide whether the residual is purchasable and say so. If durable removals cannot scale to 3.52 Mt for one airline, then "net zero 2040" needs either a different instrument or a different name.
